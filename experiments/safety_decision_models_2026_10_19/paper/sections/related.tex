\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Public benchmarks for undesired content, jailbreaks, and exaggerated refusals give the field shared tasks and labels, but they were built for different model families and scoring rules.
\citeauthor{hartvigsen2022toxigen} introduce ToxiGen for implicit and machine-generated hate \cite{hartvigsen2022toxigen}.
\citeauthor{markov2023holistic} describe the holistic moderation taxonomy that underlies the OpenAI Moderation evaluation set \cite{markov2023holistic}.
ToxicChat stresses toxicity and jailbreak cues in real user and assistant logs \cite{lin2023toxicchat}.
The deepset prompt-injection corpus offers a compact binary injection task \cite{deepset2024promptinjections}.
WildGuard and WildGuardMix extend moderation to jailbreaks, refusals, and prompt harm with an adversarial flag \cite{han2024wildguard}.
Do-Not-Answer measures whether safeguards should refuse risky questions without a benign contrast class \cite{wang2024donotanswer}.
XSTest targets exaggerated safety on prompts that are safe versus harmful by construction \cite{rottger2024xstest}.
HarmBench standardizes automated red-teaming and refusal behavior \cite{mazeika2024harmbench}, and JailbreakBench pairs harmful goals with benign look-alikes for miss and false-positive measurement \cite{chao2024jailbreakbench}.
Together these sets cover toxicity, category moderation, injections, broad harm, and over-refusal, yet prior work rarely scores them under one input-screening protocol.

Emotion-labeled text is adjacent to safety screening because wellbeing products must not treat ordinary distress as policy violations.
GoEmotions provides fine-grained emotion labels in short comments \cite{demszky2020goemotions}.
Work on contextualized affect representations, such as CARER, highlights how emotion cues depend on surrounding text \cite{saravia2018carer}.
Benchmarks in the Jev line therefore include emotion classification alongside safety sets so that block rates on non-harm emotional language can be read next to toxicity scores rather than in isolation.

A second line of work scores \emph{typed} decision models that answer fixed questions with probabilities instead of generating moderation rationales.
TypeSafe documents the Jev request shape and model versions \cite{typesafe2026jevmodels}.
Deu{\ss}er, Sparrenberg, and Sifa benchmark Jev \texttt{jev-1.13.0} zero-shot on dozens of Hugging Face tasks, including the safety and emotion sets that overlap our pack \cite{deusser2026jevbenchmark}.
Their public harness and task definitions \cite{appliedml2026jevbenchmarking} pin dataset configs, question primitives, and primary metrics such as ToxiGen accuracy, mean category AUPRC on OpenAI Moderation, ToxicChat F1 splits, prompt-injection accuracy, and GoEmotions macro-F1.
That study does not call a managed guardrail or combine a decision model with cloud policy APIs.
Before our full evaluation we reran the harness on six overlapping datasets (ToxiGen, OpenAI Moderation, ToxicChat, prompt injections, GoEmotions, and Emotion); every observed primary fell inside or matched the published intervals reported in our results log, so subsequent arms share the same templates and splits.

Cloudflare publishes Clef and Clef-flash as open-weight models that accept the same \texttt{state} and \texttt{questions} interface as Jev \cite{cloudflare2026clef,cloudflare2026clefflash}.
They are natural comparators for API-hosted typed screening, but prior public numbers are model cards and documentation rather than a multi-benchmark table aligned with Deu{\ss}er et al.

Managed guardrails implement a different interface: category filters return ordinal confidence bands and a block bit at a chosen strength rather than a calibrated harm probability.
Amazon Bedrock Guardrails documents six input content filters (Hate, Insults, Sexual, Violence, Misconduct, and Prompt Attack) and strength rules that map bands to block decisions \cite{aws2024bedrockguardrails}.
Bands are not probabilities, so calibration metrics from the scoring literature do not transfer directly.
Dataset harm labels also need an explicit crosswalk to filter names, which matters most on jailbreak benchmarks where only a subset of filters is semantically native.

When decision models do emit probabilities, calibration and selective prediction provide standard tools for threshold choice and coverage trade-offs.
\citeauthor{guo2017calibration} show that modern classifiers are often miscalibrated and discuss temperature scaling and evaluation of probabilistic outputs \cite{guo2017calibration}.
\citeauthor{geifman2017selective} formalize selective classification, abstaining on low-confidence items to reduce error at a fixed coverage \cite{geifman2017selective}.
Prior Jev reporting includes tuned thresholds and selective curves on development data \cite{deusser2026jevbenchmark}; guardrail papers and product docs seldom report the same curves because the actionable object is a band, not $P(\text{harm})$.

Our study sits at the intersection of these threads.
We keep the Deu{\ss}er et al.\ question templates and metrics where datasets overlap, add Clef, Clef-flash, and Bedrock Guardrails on the same eleven public sets (including WildGuardMix, Do-Not-Answer aggregates, XSTest, HarmBench, and JailbreakBench), and harmonize miss rate, false-positive rate, and detection rate definitions across arms.
We further evaluate union, intersection, and cascade stack policies that read only saved predictions, mirroring how a product might run a typed screener before or together with a managed guardrail.
To our knowledge, no prior work reports this full matrix under one pre-registered crosswalk, threshold rule, and cost and latency accounting for every arm.
