\section{Introduction}

Products that support mental health and emotional wellbeing must screen user input before a large language model responds.
A harmful request that passes the filter can enter the session.
A false positive can refuse someone who was describing ordinary distress.
The screening layer therefore trades missed harm against over-refusal, latency, and cost.

Two families of commercial APIs implement that layer in different ways.
Typed decision models, such as TypeSafe Jev~\cite{typesafe2026jevmodels,deusser2026jevbenchmark}, take a text state and one or more fixed questions, then return probabilities or discrete choices with no free-text generation.
Cloudflare Clef and Clef-flash follow the same request shape with open-weight models~\cite{cloudflare2026clef,cloudflare2026clefflash}.
Amazon Bedrock Guardrails scores input through \texttt{ApplyGuardrail} and returns block decisions derived from ordinal bands on six content filters~\cite{aws2024bedrockguardrails}.
Bands are not probabilities, and filter names do not align one-to-one with benchmark harm categories.
Prior work benchmarks Jev across many public datasets~\cite{deusser2026jevbenchmark} but does not place managed guardrails on the same prompts under harmonized miss-rate and false-positive-rate definitions.

We run a pre-registered comparison on eleven public datasets that score prompts only.
Safety sets include ToxiGen~\cite{hartvigsen2022toxigen}, OpenAI Moderation~\cite{markov2023holistic}, ToxicChat~\cite{lin2023toxicchat}, prompt injections~\cite{deepset2024promptinjections}, WildGuardMix~\cite{han2024wildguard}, Do-Not-Answer~\cite{wang2024donotanswer}, XSTest~\cite{rottger2024xstest}, HarmBench~\cite{mazeika2024harmbench}, and JailbreakBench~\cite{chao2024jailbreakbench}.
We add GoEmotions~\cite{demszky2020goemotions} and Emotion~\cite{saravia2018carer} because wellbeing products encounter emotionally loaded text that is not labeled as harm; we record whether guardrails block those rows while decision models are scored as classifiers.
We do not measure chat-model compliance or attack success against a target LLM.
For Do-Not-Answer we report detection rates and risk-area aggregates only, without reproducing question text.

Four arms are evaluated: Jev \texttt{jev-1.13.0}, Clef, Clef-flash, and Bedrock Guardrails with content filters only.
Each decision model is also combined with the guardrail under three stack policies (union, intersection, cascade) computed from saved predictions.
Union blocks if either component blocks; intersection blocks only when both block; cascade defers ambiguous model scores to the guardrail at medium strength.
These policies are deterministic functions of cached scorer output, so the same rules could be deployed at runtime without additional model calls.

Our study is organized around six research questions.
We ask whether Clef and Clef-flash move primary safety metrics relative to Jev beyond bootstrap uncertainty; how tuned thresholds and selective prediction at 80\% and 50\% coverage compare to a fixed 0.5 cutoff; what false-positive rates arise on benign prompts and on emotion-labeled text; how guardrail miss and false-positive rates vary at low, medium, and high strength under a published crosswalk; how stacks separate miss rate from false-positive rate relative to each arm alone; and what median latency and billed cost per 1{,}000 prompts each arm incurs on this pack.

Thresholds maximize harmful-class F1 on a development pool subject to benign false-positive rate at most 5\%, with JailbreakBench held at 0.5 when the labeled development pool is small.
We report tuned scores and scores at 0.5, plus expected calibration error and Brier scores where probabilities apply.
Guardrail strength uses a dataset-to-filter crosswalk fixed before test scoring; headline JailbreakBench metrics map harmful goals to Prompt Attack only.

Before scoring Clef or guardrails on the full pack, we ran the public Jev harness on six datasets that overlap Deu{\ss}er et al.~\cite{deusser2026jevbenchmark}.
Observed primaries fell inside published intervals (harness PASS), including ToxiGen accuracy 0.8755 and ToxicChat toxic F1 0.7878.
That step anchors later contrasts to a validated Jev baseline rather than an ad hoc template.

Headline results show sharp trade-offs rather than a single winning arm.
Jev attains low over-refusal on XSTest safe prompts (false-positive rate 0.0120 at tuned thresholds) and low miss on JailbreakBench harmful goals (0.0300).
The pre-registered guardrail mapping on the same JailbreakBench split misses 0.9800 of harmful goals, which shows how much the category crosswalk shapes guardrail behavior on jailbreak-style text.
A post-hoc sensitivity analysis that takes the maximum band across all content filters lowers medium-strength miss rate to 0.2300 at false-positive rate 0.1900; we treat that mapping as exploratory, not a registered endpoint.
On HarmBench, guardrails miss rate 0.2026 is lower than Jev miss rate 0.2870.
Clef is not uniformly better than Jev on safety primaries; for example, Clef ToxiGen accuracy is 0.7213 versus Jev 0.8755, while Clef exceeds Jev on some other sets in the core pack.
ToxicChat toxic F1 remains 0.7878 for tuned Jev versus guardrails miss rate 0.2624 on the same dataset under the crosswalk.
Do-Not-Answer Jev detection rate is 0.7433 versus 0.1523 for guardrails.
Guardrails assign miss rate 1.0000 on GoEmotions and Emotion in our scoring interface, which we interpret as blanket blocking of non-harm-labeled comments rather than emotion classification accuracy.
Stacking can shift the miss-versus-false-positive frontier on datasets with both classes, but on JailbreakBench the pre-registered intersection policy inherits the guardrail miss rate when the guardrail is stricter than the model.

Operationally, Jev median latency is 88.9\,ms and cost per 1{,}000 prompts is \$0.023, compared with guardrails at 359\,ms and \$0.179 per 1{,}000 prompts and Clef at \$0.230 per 1{,}000 prompts; total study spend was \$18.38 at pinned 2026-10-19 rates.

\textbf{Contributions.}
(1)~A harmonized benchmark of three typed decision APIs and Bedrock Guardrails on eleven prompt-level datasets, with explicit miss rate, false-positive rate, and detection-rate semantics.
(2)~A harness-validated Jev baseline aligned to prior published evaluations~\cite{deusser2026jevbenchmark}.
(3)~Pre-registered guardrail crosswalk and stack policies, plus calibration and selective-prediction analyses at tuned and 0.5 thresholds.
(4)~Documented cost and latency for the same prediction rows, and a clearly labeled post-hoc JailbreakBench guardrail sensitivity analysis.
